Is there a relationship between hypertension and stroke? $$$ **Introduction**\nHypertension is a well‑established risk factor for many vascular events, most notably stroke. The evidence base for this relationship comes from large cohort studies, meta‑analyses of randomized trials, and detailed pharmacologic investigations that quantify how reductions in blood pressure translate into stroke risk reduction.\n**Key Findings**\n1. **Quantitative Risk Reduction**\n- Meta‑analysis of 147 randomized trials (≈464 000 participants) showed that a 10 mm Hg decrease in systolic blood pressure (SBP) or a 5 mm Hg decrease in diastolic blood pressure (DBP) reduces stroke incidence by roughly 33 % (≈1/3).\n- The proportional reduction is essentially the same across the five main drug classes (thiazides, β‑blockers, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, calcium‑channel blockers), indicating that the benefit is driven by blood‑pressure lowering rather than class‑specific pleiotropic effects.\n2. **Age‑Specific Effects**\n- Age‑specific regression slopes allow calculation of relative risk for any age and BP change. For individuals aged 60–69, a 20 mm Hg SBP drop yields a relative risk of 0.43 for stroke (57 % reduction). Larger drops produce proportionally greater risk reductions (e.g., a 40 mm Hg drop gives a relative risk of ~0.18, an 82 % reduction).\n3. **Baseline Blood‑Pressure Independence**\n- The relative risk reduction per unit BP lowering is consistent regardless of baseline BP, even down to SBP of 110 mm Hg and DBP of 70 mm Hg. This supports a “lower‑the‑better” approach: any clinically achievable BP reduction confers benefit.\n4. **Special Class Effects**\n- β‑blockers provide an additional ~30 % reduction in coronary heart disease events for a few years after myocardial infarction, but this does not alter the stroke‑risk reduction.\n- Calcium‑channel blockers show a modest extra benefit for stroke (≈33 % vs ≈27 % for other classes), though the difference is small.\n5. **Population‑Level Impact**\n- Hypertension affects ≈1.3 billion adults worldwide, with uncontrolled cases causing 7.6–9 million premature deaths annually. Systolic BP ≥ 140 mm Hg accounts for about 70 % of global cardiovascular mortality, underscoring the public‑health importance of BP control.\n**Clinical Implications**\n- **Universal BP‑Lowering Strategy** – Because the proportional benefit is independent of baseline BP, clinicians should consider initiating BP‑lowering therapy in all adults above a certain age (e.g., ≥60 years) or with any elevated BP, rather than restricting treatment to those with “high” readings alone.\n- **Targeted BP Goals** – Aiming for a 10 mm Hg SBP reduction (or 5 mm Hg DBP) can be a practical, evidence‑based target to achieve a ~30 % stroke risk reduction.\n- **Drug Choice** – Any of the main antihypertensive classes can be used; choice may be guided by comorbidities, tolerability, and cost. Calcium‑channel blockers may offer a slight stroke‑specific advantage, while β‑blockers are particularly beneficial in recent myocardial infarction survivors.\n- **Monitoring and Adherence** – Regular BP monitoring and adherence support are essential, given the clear link between BP control and stroke prevention.\n**Conclusion**\nThe evidence from large randomized trials and cohort studies unequivocally demonstrates a strong, dose‑dependent relationship between hypertension and stroke. Lowering systolic blood pressure by 10 mm Hg (or diastolic by 5 mm Hg) reduces stroke risk by about one‑third, regardless of baseline BP or prior cardiovascular disease. These findings support aggressive, universal BP‑lowering strategies to mitigate stroke risk across diverse patient populations. $$$ **1.
