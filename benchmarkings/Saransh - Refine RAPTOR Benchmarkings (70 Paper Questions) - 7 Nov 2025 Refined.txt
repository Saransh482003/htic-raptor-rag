What is hypertension and how do I know if I have it? $$$ **Hypertension – Definition and How to Recognise It**\n---\nHypertension (high blood pressure) is a chronic, systemic disorder in which the arterial pressure remains persistently above normal limits.  It is the most common non‑communicable disease worldwide, affecting roughly 1.13 billion adults in 2019 and contributing to a large share of cardiovascular and renal morbidity.  Because most people with high blood pressure are asymptomatic, routine screening and objective measurement are essential for early detection and prevention of complications.\n| What hypertension is | How to know if you have it |\n|-----------------------|---------------------------|\n| **Clinical definition** – A sustained systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mm Hg, or the use of antihypertensive medication for a documented elevation. | **Diagnostic thresholds** – The International Society of Hypertension and the European Society of Cardiology/European Society of Hypertension (ESH/ESC) guidelines (reflected in the 2018 ESC/ESH position paper) set the same cut‑offs: SBP ≥ 140 mm Hg or DBP ≥ 90 mm Hg on at least two separate occasions. |\n| **Pathophysiology** – A complex interplay of vascular dysfunction, neuro‑hormonal activation (especially the renin‑angiotensin‑aldosterone system), and immune‑mediated inflammation. | **Measurement** – Accurate, reproducible readings are the cornerstone of diagnosis.  The 2018 ESC/ESH guidelines recommend: 1) a validated automated cuff, 2) seated, rested for ≥ 5 min, 3) arm at heart level, 4) two readings 1–2 min apart, and 5) the mean of the two as the office value. |\n| **Epidemiology** – 1.13 billion people worldwide were estimated to have hypertension in 2019.  Awareness is low (≈ 54 % of adults are unaware of their condition). | **Screening** – Routine measurement in primary‑care visits, annual health checks, or during any encounter that involves blood‑pressure assessment is recommended.  The 2018 ESC/ESH guidelines also endorse opportunistic screening in high‑risk groups (elderly, obese, black, or those with a family history). |\n| **Risk factors** – Modifiable: obesity, high sodium intake, physical inactivity, excessive alcohol, and smoking.  Non‑modifiable: age, sex (men earlier, women later after menopause), ethnicity (African‑American and some South‑Asian groups have higher prevalence), and genetics. | **Clinical clues** – While hypertension is often “silent,” certain symptoms may raise suspicion: headaches, visual blurring, shortness of breath, chest pain, palpitations, or a feeling of pressure in the chest.  The internal guideline lists chest pain, palpitations, and visual disturbances as red‑flag symptoms that should prompt a blood‑pressure check. |\n| **White‑coat and masked hypertension** – White‑coat hypertension is a false‑positive elevation in the clinic; masked hypertension is a false‑negative that only appears when measured outside the office.
